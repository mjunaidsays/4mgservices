Perishable cargo is the only freight where the deadline is biological. A container of machinery that clears three days late is an inconvenience. A container of chilled produce that clears three days late may be worthless.

What surprises most importers is that the spoilage rarely comes from one dramatic failure. It comes from a sequence of ordinary delays, each of which looked acceptable on its own.

## The gaps where product is lost

**Between arrival and lodgement.** If the declaration is only prepared once the vessel berths, the cargo is already waiting. On general freight that costs storage. On perishables it costs shelf life.

**Waiting for a second regulator.** Perishables usually need health, phytosanitary or drug-regulatory approval *in addition* to the customs entry. Each approval has its own queue, and they do not always run in parallel.

**During an unattended inspection.** This is the one that catches people out. A reefer selected for examination may be disconnected while it waits its turn. The inspection itself takes minutes; the wait around it can take hours, and nobody is watching the temperature unless someone is there.

**After release, before collection.** Cargo is cleared on a Friday afternoon and collected on Monday. Three days of shelf life gone for want of a booked truck.

## What actually prevents it

**Start before the cargo does.** Take the shipment details at booking, not on arrival. The declaration and the health paperwork can be prepared while the goods are still in transit — this single change removes more risk than everything else combined.

**Book the inspection in advance.** Coordinating with the health or quarantine authority before arrival turns an unpredictable wait into a scheduled appointment.

**Attend the examination.** Being physically present keeps the reefer connected and the inspection short. It is the least glamorous part of the job and one of the most valuable.

**Have the truck waiting.** Temperature-controlled onward transport should be arranged to collect the moment release is granted, not organised afterwards.

## The documents to have ready

- Commercial invoice and packing list
- Phytosanitary or health certificate
- Certificate of origin
- Temperature log or reefer data, where required
- Product registration or import permit for regulated goods

## Why many forwarders avoid this work

Perishable clearance is unforgiving. It requires pre-arrival preparation, relationships with more than one regulator, someone willing to stand at an inspection, and transport on standby. It does not scale neatly, and it goes badly wrong when it goes wrong at all.
